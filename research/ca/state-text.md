**What the law requires.** Most of California's sewer systems are covered by the State Water Board's Statewide Sanitary Sewer Systems General Order (Order WQ 2022-0103-DWQ, in force since June 2023), which sorts spills by where the sewage went. A Category 1 spill is a spill of any volume that reaches surface water, or a storm drain that discharges to surface water without the sewage being recovered. Category 2 (1,000 gallons or more) and Category 3 (50 to 999 gallons) spills do not reach surface water; Category 4 spills, under 50 gallons, are reported only as monthly counts. The operator must tell the California Office of Emergency Services within two hours of a spill of 1,000 gallons or more to the state's waters, file a draft report of each Category 1 or 2 spill in the Board's database within three business days, and certify the report, with the spill's end and the receiving water, within 15 days of the end; Category 3 spills are certified monthly. Whoever causes a sewage discharge must also tell the local health officer immediately. The law puts the duty to warn the public on the county health officer: after a known untreated sewage release the officer must immediately close and post the affected beach until the source is stopped, and keep a telephone hotline of closed and posted beaches. California law sets no period after a spill during which the water is to be avoided.

**What is published.** Each night the State Water Board publishes a file of every Category 1, 2 and 3 spill reported under the General Order, with the operator, the place, a map point, the start time, the volume that left the sewer and the volume that reached surface water, and, once the report is certified, the end time and the receiving water. On 4 November 2026 it held 3,567 spills since June 2023: 1,284 in Category 1, 459 in Category 2 and 1,824 in Category 3. In 2026 a Category 1 spill's draft report was filed a median of three days after the spill began (nine days or more for one in ten). San Francisco's sewer utility shows on its beach map, for each of 20 beach stations, when a combined sewer discharge has occurred within the last 24 to 72 hours, and which beaches are posted. The State Water Board also publishes the counties' beach postings and closures (Beach Watch) and reports of harmful algal blooms in lakes and rivers with their advisory levels.

**What is not published.** San Francisco's and Sacramento's combined sewer overflows are not in the statewide file: they are reported under the cities' own permits. Sacramento's notices go to downstream water agencies by phone, e-mail or fax, and no public web notice was found. Operators whose systems are not covered by the General Order, among them several privately owned plants, report spills only to the Regional Water Board and the health officer, and those reports are not published. The notices to health officers are not published, and the counties' current beach closures are on a telephone hotline and signs, with web pages in some counties only. Spills from privately owned sewer laterals are reported to the Board voluntarily and are not shown here. The Beach Watch compilation is months behind: on 4 November 2026 its newest advisory was dated 6 March 2026.

**What stands in the way.** The only public channel the law names is a hotline and signs at the beach; the spill reports themselves become public only through the Board's database, days after a spill and without the end time until the report is certified. The Board's spill maps refuse automated readers, though its files do not. The Office of Emergency Services, which receives the two-hour notice, publishes spill releases on a dashboard that needs a browser.

**Known problems in the data.** A draft report need not give an end time: on 4 November 2026, 175 reports were still drafts or in progress, some of them for years, and most submitted drafts (60 of 73) gave no end. In 91 reports the end typed is earlier than the start. About one Category 1 report in ten names no receiving water. Some map points were typed without the minus sign of the longitude. The location is often a home's street address.

**What this site does about it.** Category 1 spills are shown from their reported start to their reported end, then with the site's caution (72 hours; 96 on lakes, ponds and reservoirs), since the law sets no period. A report with no end, or with an end earlier than its start, is shown as having no end reported: caution for 14 days from the report, then unresolved to 30 days after the spill began. Category 2 and 3 spills are kept on the record and counted, but not placed on a water, because the report says the sewage did not reach one. San Francisco's combined sewer discharge sign is shown as an advisory in effect for as long as the utility shows it, then with the site's caution. Beach Watch postings and closures are shown as the counties' advisories; because the compilation runs months behind, one it still lists as open is shown as in effect for 14 days from the compilation's latest date and then as not re-confirmed, never as lifted. Algal bloom advisories are shown as blooms, not as sewage. The house numbers in spill locations are removed, a spill that appeared only on private property is mapped to within about 100 meters, and so is where the sewage went, and the report's narrative fields and certifier are not reproduced; the full report is in the Board's database.
