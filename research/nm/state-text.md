**What the law requires.** New Mexico does not issue its own discharge permits: the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency issues them, and each permit requires a report to the agency within 24 hours of a sewer overflow or a treatment plant bypass, and in writing within five days. State rule 20.6.2.1203 NMAC also requires the person in charge of a facility to tell the New Mexico Environment Department by telephone within 24 hours of a discharge that may with reasonable probability injure health, animal or plant life, or property, in writing within a week, and to report the corrective action within 15 days. No New Mexico statute or rule requires the public to be told when a discharge happens, and the law sets no period after a discharge during which the water is to be avoided. New Mexico has no combined sewers. On tribal land, including the Navajo Nation, the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency issues the permits and receives the reports, and the state rule does not apply. New Mexico has no state beach monitoring program; the Department issues harmful algal bloom advisories under its own guidance.

**What is published.** The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency publishes the overflow and bypass reports that permittees file electronically. Its extract of November 4, 2026 holds 2 reports under New Mexico permits, both the Town of Taos's (February 19 and October 16, 2026), and 4 reports under the Navajo Tribal Utility Authority's permits located in New Mexico: 3 from the Shiprock plant, June 2024 to November 2025, and a sewer line washed out by rain near Window Rock in August 2026. Each gives a start and an end to the minute, a map point and a volume, and reached the table a median of four days after it began. This site reads one of the six as having reached a water: a 300,000-gallon bypass of part of the treatment at the Shiprock plant from June 15 to June 22, 2024, announced in advance, whose report names the San Juan River. The agency's table lists the sewer systems of 55 New Mexico permits, Albuquerque's, Santa Fe's and Las Cruces's among them, but no other has filed a report. For algal blooms, the Department's advisory map shows the latest result at about 40 lakes and river stations (on November 7, 2026, a WARNING advisory at six lakes, among them Eagle Nest, Quemado, El Vado and Bluewater, and a WATCH at eight stations, three of them on the Rio Grande near Taos), and its news releases announce advisories; the Bureau of Land Management posts its own notice for the Rio Grande del Norte National Monument.

**What is not published.** The Department does not post the notices it receives under 20.6.2.1203, and reports sent to the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency other than through its electronic tool are not published either; both reach the public only through a records request. No e-mail or text alert for sewage discharges was found from the state or the agencies. The Albuquerque Bernalillo County Water Utility Authority, Santa Fe, Rio Rancho and Farmington give no notice of overflows on their sites; the other operators' own pages were not searched one by one. No public bacteria results for the Rio Grande through Albuquerque were found.

**What stands in the way.** The only legal channel is a telephone call to the state, and nothing reaches the public unless an operator files electronically with the federal agency, which few do although the federal deadline for electronic reporting passed in December 2025. A missing report cannot be told apart from a month without discharges. Las Cruces's website refuses automated reading. The Department's bloom map is published as a new data service each season, so its address can change without notice.

**Known problems in the data.** The federal reports give times without a time zone, and this site reads them as Mountain time; one Taos report gives a time five hours from the time its own description states. One Shiprock report gives its longitude without the minus sign, which would put it in Asia; this site reads it as west. The Navajo Nation's permits do not name a state, since the Nation spans Arizona, New Mexico and Utah. The bloom map gives each station's latest sample only, not the day an advisory began, and writes its dates in several forms.

**What this site does about it.** A report is shown from its start to its end, then with this site's caution (72 hours; 96 on lakes, ponds and reservoirs), since the law sets no period. A report that says the sewage stayed on land, reached no water or went only to the plant's own lagoon is not shown on this site; the federal record has it. A Navajo Nation report is shown in the state where its own map point lies, on the Nation's clock, which is New Mexico's Mountain time. Times are shown in Mountain time. Algal bloom advisories are shown apart from sewage, as the Department's own WATCH, WARNING or DANGER advisories, from the station's latest sample until 14 days after the Department last confirmed the advisory on its map, since the map gives no end. House numbers and personal details in the reports' descriptions are not reproduced here; the full report is in the federal records.
