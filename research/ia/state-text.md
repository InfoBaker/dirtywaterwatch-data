**What the law requires.** An unanticipated bypass or upset at a wastewater system must be reported to the Iowa Department of Natural Resources field office by telephone within 24 hours of its onset or discovery and in writing within five days (567 IAC 63.6). The Department decides whether the public must be told; when it does, the operator prepares the notice. No Iowa statute or rule sets a period after a sewage discharge during which the water is to be avoided. Nine Iowa systems hold permits for combined sewer overflows (Burlington, Clinton, Des Moines, Fort Madison, Keokuk, Muscatine, Ottumwa, Spencer and Wapello); public notification of overflows is one of the federal policy's minimum controls, but no web notice from any of them was found.

**What is published.** The Department's field offices keep a public record of each report, one page per report, with the times the incident began and ended, the receiving stream, the estimated gallons and the county as the field office entered them. We read them for the 105 facilities the Department classes major municipal: on November 15, 2026, they listed 359 bypass and overflow reports from January 2025 on, from 76 facilities (125 in a collection system, 79 at a treatment plant, 155 whose place the report does not give); 351 give the start to the minute, 330 give an end and 266 an estimate of gallons. The Department issued four news releases about municipal wastewater from November 2024 to October 2026: three name a discharge (Sioux City, Story City and Keokuk), and one, on statewide flooding, names no community for its bypasses and advises staying out of flooded streams. The City of Waterloo posts each sewer overflow and bypass on its own page: 17 notices from October 2025 to October 2026, each with its street and a time (the start, or for six the time the City was notified), 15 with a duration.

**What is not published.** The field-office reports of the other municipal and semi-public facilities (about 930) are public in the same application but not read yet. The Department's beach monitoring results are published only from Memorial Day to Labor Day and were empty in November; advisories at the Corps of Engineers' reservoir beaches are given by telephone. The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency's overflow records hold no Iowa event: Iowa's reports go to the Department, not to EPA's system.

**What stands in the way.** The field-office application gives no list or download of reports: each report is a separate page, so we read new reports on later refreshes in turn. None of the nine combined sewer systems publishes its overflows on the web. Ten channels have no web page and two are by telephone only. The fix is a public list or export of the field offices' reports and a duty to post combined sewer overflows.

**Known problems in the data.** The receiving stream is typed by the field office: 225 of the 359 reports name none, four give "None", "Varies" or "unknown", one says "multiple, see report", and others abbreviate ("Mississippi", "Cedar") or misspell a water. Gallons are the operator's estimate. 29 reports give no end and 8 no start that can be read. The news releases give dates only in their text, and a release's own date is not a start.

**What this site does about it.** A report is placed by the receiving stream it names, within the county the report gives; the treatment plant's point is never used as the report's place, and a report that names no water is listed with its operator and county without a marker. A report with an end is shown while it ran and then with our 72-hour caution; a report with no end is never shown as continuing, and one dated only to the day takes the caution from that day. A report's operator is the permit holder whose treatment plant serves the facility the Department names (349 of the 359). The 39 combined sewer outfalls the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency lists as open, across the nine permits, carry our rain caution; the 45 it lists as closed do not. Dates are read in Central time.
