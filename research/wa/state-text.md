**What the law requires.** Since 2025, Washington law (RCW 90.48.636) requires the Department of Ecology itself to publish every sewage spill reported to it under an individual water quality permit, on a public website that went live in mid-2026. Each notice gives the estimated volume, the level of treatment, when and where the spill began, when it ended once known, the area potentially affected and the steps taken to contain it, with final information added after the spill ends. The law defines a sewage spill as untreated or undertreated wastewater that does not meet permit requirements, combined sewer overflows included. It sets no deadline for posting; Ecology aims to post within five working days of the operator's report. Operators report their spills to Ecology under their permits. The law sets no period after a spill during which the water is to be avoided. Combined sewer operators also owe the public notice under their permits: King County and Seattle Public Utilities publish a status map for their outfalls and post signs at them, and advise staying out of the water near an outfall for 48 hours after it overflows.

**What is published.** Ecology's list of sewage spills, from June 2026, with the facility, the county, the start and end to the minute, the volume, the level of treatment, the location as a street block or intersection, the area the operator says was potentially affected and a link to the operator's report. On 4 November 2026 it held 72 reports: 60 sewer overflows, 9 discharges of partly treated wastewater from treatment plants, one plant bypass of untreated sewage, one combined sewer overflow and one report with no type. King County's status map gives every 10 minutes whether each of 125 combined sewer outfalls in Seattle (36 King County, 89 Seattle) is overflowing now, overflowed in the last 48 hours, has had no recent overflow, or has no data; the county notes that the real-time data has not been reviewed for accuracy. EPA publishes the spill reports of Joint Base Lewis-McChord, whose permit EPA issues.

**What is not published.** Ecology's list gives no map point and no permit number, and names the facility, not always the operator. It names a receiving water only when the operator's description of the area does: of the 72 reports, 18 name a water, 34 say the sewage stayed on land, in a building or did not reach water, and 20 name a storm drain or give no answer. The time Ecology received or posted a report is not given. King County's map gives no start or end time, no volume and no receiving water, and keeps no history: only the status now. Spills that are not reported under an individual permit, such as a backup from a private line, are not on the list. Spokane's real-time combined sewer page refuses automated reading, so Spokane's overflows are seen here only when they are reported to Ecology. There is no e-mail or text alert for sewage spills: the notification system in the original bill was not enacted.

**What stands in the way.** The law sets no posting deadline, so a report can appear days after the spill. The alert system, map format and annual report were cut from the bill as passed. Spokane's combined sewer page sits behind a firewall that refuses automated reading. Ecology's list does not carry a map point or a permit number, which would let each report be placed on its water without guesswork.

**Known problems in the data.** Several reports give an end before their start, and some starts are typed as midnight, which may be a date given without a time. Volumes are typed as text, sometimes as ranges or rates. The location is typed by the operator, and the area potentially affected is free text. King County's file shows five Seattle outfalls at a placeholder point far from Seattle. EPA's national inventory lists most of King County's and Seattle's combined sewer outfalls as closed, while the county's status map reports them every 10 minutes.

**What this site does about it.** A report with an end is shown from its start to its end; a report with no end yet is shown as ongoing for seven days and then as unresolved, with no end reported, until 30 days after it began; an end before the start is not used. With no period in law, this site applies its own caution after a reported end (72 hours; 96 on lakes, ponds and reservoirs). A report that says the sewage did not reach water, or stayed in a building or on land, is kept but not placed on a water. Discharges of partly treated wastewater from a treatment plant, including disinfection failures, are shown as sewage reports, since the law counts them as sewage spills. For King County's and Seattle's outfalls, this site reads the status map every hour: an overflow is dated to the map times at which it was first and last seen, and the operators' own 48 hours apply after it. An overflow that began and ended between two readings is shown with no start. The outfalls are placed at the map's points rather than EPA's closed records. The operators' descriptions of cleanup are not reproduced here, because they can name the people involved and residents' homes; the full report is linked from Ecology's list.
