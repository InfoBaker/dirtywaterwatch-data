**What the law requires.** The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency writes Puerto Rico's sewage permits. Their standard conditions require the permittee to report an overflow, bypass or other discharge that may endanger health or the environment to the agency by telephone within 24 hours and in writing within five days, now filed electronically (40 CFR 122.41(l)(6) and part 127). No Puerto Rico statute or rule requiring the public to be told of a sewage overflow or bypass was found, and no period after a discharge is set during which the water is to be avoided. Puerto Rico's one public sewer operator is the Puerto Rico Aqueduct and Sewer Authority (Autoridad de Acueductos y Alcantarillados, PRASA). Its Puerto Nuevo system in San Juan has the island's only combined sewers (sewage and rainwater in one pipe), with 12 outfalls on the Caño Martín Peña, the Río de Puerto Nuevo and San Juan Bay; that permit (PR0021555, 2021) requires "a public notification process to inform citizens of when and where CSOs occur", and the 2023 modification of PRASA's federal consent decree adds posting of those discharges and their estimated volume on PRASA's website. A Senate investigation opened in 2025 (R. del S. 310) proposes weekly public reports of incidents. The Department of Natural and Environmental Resources (Departamento de Recursos Naturales y Ambientales, DRNA) runs Puerto Rico's beach monitoring and public notification program under the federal BEACH Act.

**What is published.** The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency publishes the reports Puerto Rico's permittees file, daily: 940 reports from 45 permits in its file of November 12, 2026, all of them PRASA's, begun between April 3, 2025 and November 10, 2026 (863 sewer overflows and 77 treatment plant bypasses), each with a map point; all but seven give start and end times to the minute (three storm reports give only the days of the report period) and all but eight a volume. The file records the day EPA received each report, a median of two days after the discharge began and 14 days or more for one report in ten; it does not say when each report was published. DRNA publishes a notice about once a week listing the beaches whose latest samples exceed the federal Beach Action Value for enterococci bacteria (70 colonies per 100 millilitres), which it declares "no son aptas para bañistas" (not fit for bathers): 280 notices from October 11, 2019 to November 13, 2026, for 36 monitored beaches, each giving the days the samples were taken (275 of the 277 readable notices give a date, a median of three days before the notice), a median of seven days apart. The notice of November 13, 2026 lists Tropical Beach in Naguabo. PRASA's website maps its 12 combined sewer outfalls and gives precautions and a form for the public to report an overflow.

**What is not published.** No list of overflows comes from PRASA or from Puerto Rico's own agencies: the reports go to the federal agency, and copies sent to DRNA are not published. No discharge from the Puerto Nuevo combined sewers is posted anywhere this site could find, although the permit and the consent decree call for public notice; none of the federal reports is filed as a combined sewer overflow. DRNA's notices name only the beaches over the value: they give no sample results, no time an advisory is lifted, and no map. The San Juan Bay Estuary Program and Surfrider Foundation volunteers sample bacteria at some beaches and lagoons; whether either publishes a current list was not verified (only the programs' pages were found).

**What stands in the way.** Apart from the Puerto Nuevo permit's duty to notify the public of its combined sewer overflows, which has no web channel, no rule requires the public to be told, so an overflow becomes public only through the federal file, days or weeks later, and only when the operator files it electronically. DRNA's notices are posted without stated terms of use, and a week can pass, or several, between them: the gap was 109 days in the spring of 2020 and over 30 days eight times in all.

**Known problems in the data.** The federal reports give times without a time zone; this site reads them in Atlantic Standard Time, which Puerto Rico keeps all year. Reports are written in English, Spanish or both, so one river appears as "RIO BAIROA" and "BAIROA RIVER", and the sea as "OCEANO ATLANTICO" or "Caribe Ocean"; 73 of the reports this site shows say a creek, channel or ravine was reached without naming it ("unnamed creek", "CREEK (UNKNOWN THE NAME)"). The description field names the pump station or plant, not what happened. Most reports, 688 of the 940, name no water and report only odours, appearance, land or no observable effect. DRNA's notices sometimes give a beach's station number wrongly (Playa Cabullón, Ponce, listed under Guayanés's number) or misspell names, and two give their sampling days in a form that names no date.

**What this site does about it.** A federal report is shown from its start to its end, then with this site's caution (72 hours; 96 on lakes, ponds and reservoirs), since the law sets no period. A report is shown when it names a water, says a water was reached without naming it, reports an effect on a water (aquatic habitat or life, a beach, an algal bloom or eutrophication) or names the storm drain system; the other 688 are not shown on this site, and the federal record has every report. A report is placed at its own map point when that point is the pump station or other structure where the overflow happened (146 of the 252 shown). A treatment plant's point is not where a discharge reached the water, so a bypass, a report naming a treatment plant and a report whose point lies within a kilometre of the plant's point are placed by the water they name once that water is on this site's map, or not placed and listed (106); no place is guessed from the text. A water named in a report is shown as filed, never translated. DRNA's notices are read at every refresh: each beach a notice lists is shown as DRNA's own advisory, in DRNA's words, from the time DRNA published the first notice that listed it. A notice saying the beaches are fit for bathers ends it at its publication. A later notice that simply no longer lists the beach is not taken as a lift, since DRNA states none: the advisory is shown for 14 days after the last notice that listed it and its end is marked as seen at that later notice (408 of the 601 advisories read; 192 ended by a notice that the beaches are fit). A notice DRNA later revises keeps what it first said, and a beach a revision adds is shown from the revision. The sampling days are shown apart and never as the start. A beach that DRNA does not list, a notice that the program's beaches are fit for bathers, and a green flag at a beach are never shown as a passing result here: such a beach has no beach advisory record (another kind of report may still apply there). Each beach is marked at the monitoring station's own point; a beach a notice lists that cannot be matched to DRNA's station table stays in the list with no place on the map, and the list says why. The 12 Puerto Nuevo outfalls are mapped from PRASA's own layer. The rain-based caution this site computes elsewhere is not computed for Puerto Rico: the radar rainfall it uses covers only the contiguous United States. House numbers, telephone numbers and names in the reports' text are not reproduced here.
