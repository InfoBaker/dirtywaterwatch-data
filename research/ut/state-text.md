**What the law requires.** Utah's sewer management rule requires the owner of a sewer system to report a Class 1 sanitary sewer overflow to the Division of Water Quality by telephone within 24 hours and in writing within five calendar days (Utah Admin. Code R317-801-4). A Class 1 overflow is any that reaches waters of the state, exceeds 5,000 gallons, affects more than five private buildings or any public, commercial or industrial building, or may be a risk to public health (R317-801-1(13)); smaller overflows go only in the system's annual report, due April 15. No Utah law or rule requires the public to be told of an overflow, and none sets a period after one during which the water is to be avoided. Utah has no combined sewers. Local health departments, with the Division, post advisories for harmful algal blooms and E. coli at monitored lakes, reservoirs and streams.

**What is published.** The Department of Environmental Quality publishes the log of incidents phoned in to its spill hotline since 1991 as a public map layer, edited daily, with a map point and a report for each incident: 8,980 incidents of every kind on November 8, 2026, of which 387 are sewage reports by their titles (sewer overflows, treatment plant and lagoon releases, septic releases, and dumping of sewage, septage and human waste), 20 to 38 a year since 2017 and 25 in 2026 to November 7. The log gives the time each incident was discovered (stored in UTC), the county and the nearest town. The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency's overflow records, which Utah's permittees can file electronically, held one Utah report on November 4, 2026: a bypass line struck by a vehicle at American Fork on August 17, 2026 (about 1,000 gallons, ten minutes), received eight days later. The Division's Recreational Waters Map shows the current advisory at each of 167 monitored sites, weekly in season: on November 7, 2026, 64 sites under an algal bloom or algae mat advisory, 12 under an E. coli advisory, and 8 where the Division's monitoring team observed a few harmful algae mats, which its map marks as "Mats CF" and does not count among its advisory levels.

**What is not published.** The Class 1 reports operators send the Division are not published anywhere; an overflow appears in the hotline log only when someone also calls the hotline, so most Class 1 overflows are missing from it. The log gives no end time, no volume, no operator and no receiving water except where its title names one (20 of the 387); its description of each incident names the people involved and private addresses and is not used here. EPA lists 188 Utah sewer systems that may file electronically, and only one has. The Recreational Waters Map shows only advisories in effect, not when they were lifted.

**What stands in the way.** There is no public list of the reports the law requires, and no duty to tell the public; a records request to the Division is the only way to see them. The Department's older incident search site failed with an expired certificate in November 2026.

**Known problems in the data.** The hotline log has no field for the material released, so sewage reports are picked by the words of their titles; titles that say only "wastewater" (61 of the 387) are kept as sewage, and industrial wastewater, indoor backups and other materials are left out. Before 2013 the log titled an incident by the party involved, so 9 older titles name a sewer district or business, not what was released. 39 of the sewage reports carry no date. The log's date field is the date of the discovery time in UTC, so an evening discovery carries the next day: 14 of the 83 sewage reports since 2024. Map points are approximate. EPA's one Utah report gives its longitude without the minus sign.

**What this site does about it.** A sewage report from the hotline log is placed by its map point on the water within 400 metres, or on the water its title names; otherwise it is listed with its county and placed on no water, never at a plant chosen by its town. A report is dated to the day it was discovered in Mountain time, the earlier of the log's date and the Mountain-time date of its discovery time. Because the log gives no end, a report is shown with this site's caution for 14 days from the start of that day, then with its end not confirmed until 30 days after; a report whose title reads as a dumping, an RV, a portable toilet, human waste or a complaint is marked as the public's report, since the log does not say who called or whether an authority confirmed it, and is shown with this site's caution only. The overflow EPA also holds is shown once, from EPA's record with its times and volume; the public's reports and reports naming another water are never merged with EPA's. An algal bloom or E. coli advisory is shown as the health department's own advisory for as long as the Division's map shows it; a site marked "Looks Good" is shown as no report, and a site where the Division observed algae mats ("Mats CF") is shown with this site's caution. Dates are read in Mountain time. The names and addresses in the log's descriptions are not reproduced here, map points are kept to about 100 metres, and a report whose title names a private home, property or septic system is listed with its county only; the log links each full report.

As of November 8, 2026, of the 116 registered treatment-plant and sewer outfalls in Utah (closed outfalls aside), 90 (78 percent) are tied to a water record on this site and 26 (22 percent) are not yet (the water they discharge to has no record, or the location given could not be matched); Utah's reports carry their own map points, so a report is placed by its point, and one that lies on no water is listed with its county, with no water coloured.

The hotline log holds only the overflows someone called in, so this state's coverage reflects that log, not every overflow its sewer systems report.
