**What the law requires.** Arizona's rule for sewage collection systems (A.A.C. R18-9-C305) requires the operator to report a release that is an imminent and substantial threat to public health or the environment to the Arizona Department of Environmental Quality within 24 hours, and in writing within five business days. A release counts when it is more than 2,000 gallons, or more than 50 gallons that reach a stream, a street, a public area, a detention basin or drywell, or a building. Treatment plants that discharge to surface water under an Arizona (AZPDES) permit owe the same 24-hour and five-day reports under their permits. No Arizona statute or rule requires the public to be told when a release happens. The only public duty is a warning sign, in a format the Department approves, at a location where more than three reportable releases happened in 12 months, kept up until 12 months pass without one. The law sets no period after a release during which the water is to be avoided. Arizona has no combined sewers. On tribal land, including the Navajo Nation, the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency issues the permits and receives the reports, and the state rule does not apply. Arizona has no state beach monitoring program; lake managers test for algal blooms voluntarily.

**What is published.** The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency publishes the overflow and bypass reports that permittees file electronically. Its extract of November 4, 2026 holds 288 reports under Arizona permits from 42 permits, from September 2019 to October 28, 2026, and 52 reports under the Navajo Tribal Utility Authority's permits located in Arizona (Window Rock, Kayenta, Tuba City, Twin Arrows and Chinle), from February 2024 to October 20, 2026. Nearly all give a start and an end to the minute and a map point; 44 of the Arizona-permit reports give a volume. An Arizona-permit report reaches the table a median of three days after the release began, and one in ten takes 10 days or more. This site reads 55 of the 340 as having reached a water: 27 sewer overflows and 28 treatment plant bypasses, 15 of them the Navajo Nation's. The Arizona-permit reports name a receiving water on 24 reports, most of them by the Department's waterbody code, which this site turns into the water's name from the Department's own waterbody map; the Navajo Nation's name washes and creeks such as Black Creek Wash, Moenkopi Wash and Laguna Creek. For algal blooms, the National Park Service posts advisories for Lake Mead and Lake Mohave, Mohave County posts warnings for Lake Havasu among its news, and the Department keeps a list of blooms currently reported. The Town of Marana has posted its own overflows among its news.

**What is not published.** The Department does not post the reports it receives. Systems that hold only an aquifer protection permit, such as Sedona's, report their releases to the Department, but their reports reach no public record, and neither do the annual tables of releases over 50 gallons. No list of the locations that must carry a warning sign was found. No report under any of Pima County's permits, which serve the Tucson area, is in the federal table, although that table lists the county's collection systems; the county's pages offer a sewer service-request form but no notice of overflows. No e-mail or text alert for sewage releases was found from the state or the agencies; Phoenix's and Mesa's pages give no notice of overflows, and the other operators' own pages were not searched one by one.

**What stands in the way.** The public's only legal channel is a sign, and only after repeated releases at one place. The Department's reporting system is open only to account holders, and its website refuses automated reading, so its bloom list is not read here; the pages of Tempe Town Lake, Yuma's utilities and Sedona's wastewater department refuse it too. A missing report in the federal table cannot be told apart from a month without releases.

**Known problems in the data.** The federal reports give times without a time zone; this site reads the Arizona-permit reports as Arizona time, which keeps no daylight saving time, and the Navajo Nation's as the Nation's own time, which does. Many reports give the treatment plant's location rather than the release's: 30 of the 32 reports for Grand Canyon National Park's South Rim plant share one point. Most Arizona-permit reports describe the place only by a report number, and their descriptions of the impact are cut at 100 characters. Most releases reached a street, a retention basin or dry ground, and only a few reports name a receiving water.

**What this site does about it.** A report is shown from its start to its end, then with this site's caution (72 hours; 96 on lakes, ponds and reservoirs), since the law sets no period. A report that says the sewage stayed on land or in a retention basin, or reached no water, is not shown on this site; the federal record has it. A bypass whose report says the flow went to the plant's own pond or lagoon, or stayed on the plant's grounds, is not shown either. A report that says the sewage reached a wash, an arroyo or a canal is a report on that water, as is one that names a receiving water. Where three or more of a permit's reports give the same point and that point lies within a kilometre of the plant, it is taken as the plant's location, not the release's: those reports are placed by the water they name and otherwise listed without a point, while a plant bypass keeps the plant's point. A shared point farther from the plant is kept as the report gives it. Times are shown in Arizona time. Street addresses and house numbers in the reports' descriptions are not reproduced here; the full report is in the federal records.
