**What the law requires.** Montana's wastewater discharge permits require a sewer system to report an overflow, a bypass or other noncompliance that may seriously endanger health or the environment to the Department of Environmental Quality or to Disaster and Emergency Services as soon as possible and within 24 hours, and in writing within five days on the Department's sanitary sewer overflow form, which asks for the start and end, the volume, the location, the receiving water and whether the sewage reached water. No Montana statute, rule or permit found requires the public to be told of an overflow, and none sets a period after one during which the water is to be avoided. Montana has no combined sewers. Permits on tribal land are issued by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, and their reports go to it.

**What is published.** The Department's harmful algal bloom map holds the bloom reports made to it since 2019, with the Department's advisory level and the dates it issued and lifted each advisory: on November 15, 2026, 445 reports, with 314 advisories by their issue dates (58 issued in 2026, 58 in 2025; two reports hold a second advisory issued after the first was lifted), 294 of those with a lift date; no advisory was in effect at that read. The reports the public sends are on the same map; only those the Department turned into an advisory are shown here.

**What is not published.** The overflow reports operators send the Department are not published anywhere, and the Environmental Protection Agency's overflow tables (files of November 13, 2026) held no Montana report on November 15, 2026, so no Montana sewage overflow is shown here. The Department's July 2023 notice of more than three million gallons of partially treated wastewater reaching Warm Springs Creek gives no start or end for the release. No statewide beach bacteria program was found.

**What stands in the way.** There is no public list of the reports the permits require, and no duty to tell the public; a records request to the Department is the only way to see them. The Department's permit system sits behind a sign-in.

**Known problems in the data.** The map's issue and lift fields are written notes, not dates: an advisory's issue date is the earliest date in its issue note, and its lift date is the date the lift note gives for the lift itself. A sampling or test date in the lift note is not read as the lift, and a note that calls a lift pending, planned or conditional is not read as a lift. A date written without its year is read in the year of the observation. 20 advisories have no lift date although the map shows no advisory in effect. Many advisories are lifted about two weeks after issue (82 of the 294 lifts come 14 to 16 days after it), often with the note that no new information was received, which records a lapse, not a finding that the bloom has gone. The map's terms say its data is for reference and that the Department cannot verify publicly edited data.

**What this site does about it.** A bloom advisory is shown from the Department's issue date to its lift date, as the Department's own advisory; one with no lift date stays shown while the map shows it at the Caution or Closure level. Once the map no longer does, it is shown for 14 days from the last read that showed it, or from its issue date if no read did, since the Department confirms nothing after that. The notes on each report are not reproduced, since they can name the people who reported a bloom; only their dates are used. An advisory is placed by its map point and the water's name, matched only to a water in the report's county; a report at a water the reporter called private keeps its county only. Dates are read in Mountain time. The treatment-plant outfalls of 156 of Montana's 207 permit holders are shown (the federal records give no outfall for the other 51), and their reports are listed when they become public. The Department's news releases are not yet read here.
