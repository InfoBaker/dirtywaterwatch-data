**What the law requires.** Montana's wastewater discharge permits require a sewer system to report an overflow, a bypass or other noncompliance that may seriously endanger health or the environment to the Department of Environmental Quality or to Disaster and Emergency Services as soon as possible and within 24 hours, and in writing within five days on the Department's sanitary sewer overflow form, which asks for the start and end, the volume, the location, the receiving water and whether the sewage reached water. No Montana statute, rule or permit found requires the public to be told of an overflow, and none sets a period after one during which the water is to be avoided. Montana has no combined sewers. Permits on tribal land are issued by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, and their reports go to it.

**What is published.** The Department's harmful algal bloom map holds the bloom reports made to it since 2019, with the Department's advisory level and the dates it issued and lifted each advisory: on November 14, 2026, 445 reports, of which 276 carry an advisory issue date (57 in 2026, 57 in 2025), 254 of those with a lift date; no advisory was in effect at that read. The reports the public sends are on the same map; only those the Department turned into an advisory are shown here.

**What is not published.** The overflow reports operators send the Department are not published anywhere, and the Environmental Protection Agency's overflow tables held no Montana report on November 14, 2026, so no Montana sewage overflow is shown here. The Department's 2023 notice of more than three million gallons of partially treated wastewater reaching Warm Springs Creek could not be read in this build. No statewide beach bacteria program was found.

**What stands in the way.** There is no public list of the reports the permits require, and no duty to tell the public; a records request to the Department is the only way to see them. On November 14, 2026 the Department's and the Department of Public Health and Human Services' web sites did not answer this site's automated reader, so the overflow form and the permits were not read again.

**Known problems in the data.** The map's issue and lift fields are written notes, not dates: an advisory's issue date is the earliest date in its note and its lift date the earliest date in the lift note; a date written without its year is read in the year of the observation. 22 advisories have no lift date although the map shows no advisory in effect. Many advisories are lifted about two weeks after issue (73 of the 254 lifts come 14 to 16 days after it), often with the note that no new information was received, which records a lapse, not a finding that the bloom has gone. The map's terms say its data is for reference and that the Department cannot verify publicly edited data.

**What this site does about it.** A bloom advisory is shown from the Department's issue date to its lift date, as the Department's own advisory; one with no lift date is shown for 14 days from its issue, then no longer, since the Department confirms nothing after it. The notes on each report are not reproduced, since they can name the people who reported a bloom; only their dates are used. An advisory is placed by its map point and the water's name, matched only to a water in the report's county; a report at a water the reporter called private keeps its county only. Dates are read in Mountain time. The treatment-plant outfalls of Montana's 207 permit holders are shown, and their reports are listed when they become public. Not yet read here: the Department's news notices, once its site answers.
