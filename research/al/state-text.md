**What the law requires.** Alabama's rule (Ala. Admin. Code r. 335-6-6-.12(l)6.(v)) requires every sewage permit holder, public or private, to report a sanitary sewer overflow that reaches a surface water of the state, or may endanger human health, to the Alabama Department of Environmental Management, the public, the county health department and affected water systems "as soon as possible". The Department's reporting form sets the report at 24 hours, with a written follow-up within five days, both filed through its electronic permitting system. The rule names the public but sets no channel, content or deadline for that notice: in 2017 the Alabama Rivers Alliance and eight riverkeeper groups petitioned for a minimum standard (web posting, media notice and opt-in alerts), and a 2018 petition for a 12-hour window was denied. Alabama has no combined sewers (sewage and rainwater in one pipe). No period after a discharge is set during which the water should be avoided. On the coast, the Department and the Alabama Department of Public Health run the beach monitoring program under the federal BEACH Act, and the Department of Public Health issues swim advisories and lifts them when bacteria fall below the federal threshold.

**What is published.** The Department posts every sewer overflow report on a public map within minutes of filing, with the point, the start and stop to the minute, an estimated volume (a number or a range), the receiving water as the operator gives it, the permit and the permittee: 14,758 reports from May 2012 to November 6, 2026 on November 7, 2026, about 1,100 to 2,300 a year since 2017 and 1,008 so far in 2026, from 319 permits. The map shows reports still running and those that stopped in the last 10 days, with the cause and the time the Department received the latest filing; each signed report is in the Department's eFile system. Subscribers can also receive the reports by e-mail, by county. The beach program's map gives, for 25 coastal stations on the Gulf of Mexico, Mobile Bay, Perdido Bay and nearby waters, the last sample date, the results and an advisory code.

**What is not published.** The full report history gives no time of receipt, so how soon a report reached the Department can be seen only while it is on the live map. The history cuts the volume wording short ("10,000 < gall"), so the range is given in full only for recent reports. Operators' own notices to the public are not collected anywhere; Mobile Area Water and Sewer System posts its overflows as news items and Jefferson County sends opt-in alerts, but no other operator's channel was found. The beach map's advisory codes are not explained on the map or the program's page: every station showed the same code on November 7, 2026.

**What stands in the way.** With no channel set for the public notice, whether and how an operator tells the public is left to it; the Department's map fills the gap for dated reports, and its e-mail alerts and Jefferson County's need a sign-up. The Department states no terms of use for its map data.

**Known problems in the data.** The receiving water is often a statement rather than a water: about 4,100 reports say the sewage was absorbed by the ground, contained, pumped up or backed up into a building, and about 1,000 name only a storm drain or ditch, or nothing. Volumes are mostly ranges, and in about 3,800 reports the range is cut short. On November 7, 2026, ten reports were marked as still running, one since March 2018 and six from one county authority's plant. A few reports say they duplicate another report or, after investigation, were not a sewer overflow. Many locations are street addresses.

**What this site does about it.** A report is shown from its start to its stop, then with this site's caution (72 hours; 96 on lakes, ponds and reservoirs), since the law sets no period. A report the Department still marks as running is listed as such; on the map it is red for seven days from its start, then marked unresolved to day 30, and after that its page notes that no end was reported, because this site does not assume a discharge runs for months or years. A report that names a water is placed on that water (a storm drain or ditch with a named creek counts as reaching the creek; an unnamed tributary is placed on the water it flows to); a report that says the sewage reached no water is kept on the record but not placed on a water; a report that names only a storm drain or ditch, or no water, is placed on the water at its point when the point lies within 400 metres of one, or else on the water every registered outfall of its permit discharges to, since a drain leads to a water; with neither, it is listed without a water. Volumes given as ranges are shown as ranges, never as a single figure. The beach map is read hourly: since the Department does not publish what its codes mean, a station whose code differs from the code every station showed on November 7, 2026 is shown under this site's caution, not as a health department advisory, from the reading that first shows it until the code returns, and the code is quoted. House numbers in the reports' locations are not reproduced here; the Department's point and the street stay, and the full report is in the Department's records.
