**What the law requires.** Nebraska has no statute or rule that requires the public to be told of a sewage overflow or a treatment plant bypass; the state's release-notification rule covers oil and hazardous substances, not sewage. Each sewage permit requires the operator to report an overflow or bypass to the Department of Water, Energy, and Environment within 24 hours and in writing within five days; those reports are filed through the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency's electronic reporting system. Omaha, the state's one combined sewer operator (sewage and rainwater in one pipe, 25 open outfalls to the Missouri River and Papillion Creek's tributaries), must notify the public of overflows under its permit; the permit's text is held in the state's document viewer and its notification terms could not be read. The law sets no period after a discharge during which the water is to be avoided. The Department and the Department of Health and Human Services put a lake on health alert, and close its designated beach, when a weekly sample shows microcystin, a toxin of algal blooms, above 8 micrograms per litre.

**What is published.** The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency publishes the reports Nebraska operators file, daily, with the start and end to the minute, the point and in most cases the volume: 162 reports from 21 permits between April 2022 and October 2026 in its November 4, 2026 file, of which this site reads 51 as having reached a water (combined sewer overflows, sanitary sewer overflows and bypasses); most of the others (83) are sewage backups into buildings. Omaha's Papillion Creek plant files the most, 71. A report reaches that table a median of 23 days after the discharge began; one in ten takes 83 days or more. The Department's Beach Watch program samples 57 public beaches weekly from mid-May to mid-September and publishes each result with its E. coli count, microcystin level and health alert: 20 samples on health alert in the 2026 season, at seven lakes.

**What is not published.** No Nebraska agency or operator was found to publish overflow notices on the web. Omaha's combined sewer overflows have no web notice; the city announces large overflows through local news, with a map, and its program site carries projects and reports only. Only 21 of the state's 336 sewage permits have a report in the federal table (Lincoln, the second-largest city, has five), and a missing report there cannot be told apart from a quiet month. The reports to the state agency are not posted. Beach Watch gives no time an alert was lifted, and the program issues no advisory when E. coli is high.

**What stands in the way.** With no duty to tell the public, the only dated record arrives weeks after the event. Omaha's notification terms sit in a document viewer that gives no linkable copy of the permit, and the city's web pages refuse automated reading. Health alerts are also sent by e-mail list and social media, which this site does not read.

**Known problems in the data.** The federal reports give times without a time zone, and Nebraska keeps Central time in the east and Mountain time in its western counties; this site reads each report in the zone of its own location, by the county boundaries federal regulation sets (every report so far lies in the Central part). Many reports describe the place by a street address, name a basement or name no water at all; reports that say the sewage stayed out of the water, or entered a building, are kept but not placed on one. Omaha's Papillion Creek plant files its sewer overflows as combined sewer overflows; they are shown as reported.

**What this site does about it.** A report is shown from its start to its end, then with the site's caution (72 hours; 96 on lakes, ponds and reservoirs), since the law sets no period; because reports arrive weeks late, most appear after their caution has passed and are listed on the water's page. A Beach Watch health alert is shown as the agencies' advisory from the day of the sample, and a sample the program labels an elevated E. coli risk is shown with the site's caution, not as an advisory. Street addresses and house numbers in the reports' descriptions are not reproduced here; the full report is in the federal records.
