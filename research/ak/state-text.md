**What the law requires.** No Alaska statute or rule requires the public to be told of a sewer overflow or a treatment plant bypass. The operator of a wastewater system must report to the Department of Environmental Conservation within 24 hours when a spill or other event threatens public health or water quality, when an overflow occurs or when part of the treatment works is bypassed, and must send a written report within seven days (18 AAC 72.940); the state's wastewater discharge permits add a written report within five days. The law sets no period after a discharge during which the water is to be avoided. Juneau is the state's one city with combined sewers (sewage and rainwater in one pipe), with three combined sewer outfalls on Gastineau Channel: its permit requires that "the public receives adequate notification of CSO occurrences and CSO impacts", and public notice of sewer overflows that may imminently and substantially endanger human health. Its treatment plant permits also require shoreline signs at the outfalls advising against swimming and raw shellfish harvest in the mixing zone.

**What is published.** The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency publishes, daily, the overflow reports that permit holders file through its electronic reporting system, with the start and end to the minute, the point, the receiving water when one is named, and the volume. On November 4, 2026 that table held 46 Alaska reports from three permits, filed from June 2023 to October 2026: 43 from the Anchorage Water and Wastewater Utility, two planned pump-station bypasses in Ketchikan and one overflow at Denali National Park. A report reached the table a median of one day after the discharge began. Five of the 46 name a water (Fish Creek, Birch Lake and Cook Inlet in Anchorage, Tongass Narrows in Ketchikan) and a sixth names a place on Riley Creek in Denali National Park; 23 describe sewage backing up into a building. The City and Borough of Juneau lists its combined sewer overflow notices on its wastewater utility page; the last is from November 2005, and the page states that no combined sewer overflow has occurred since 2005.

**What is not published.** The reports operators make to the Department of Environmental Conservation are not posted by the department, and only three of about one hundred Alaska sewage discharge permits in the federal permit records have filed overflow reports in the federal table; for the others no report is published anywhere. Juneau's combined sewer overflows do not appear in the federal table, and the overflow notices its permit requires for sanitary sewers were not found on the web. The department's beach monitoring pages could not be read, so whether it publishes results or advisories is not known.

**What stands in the way.** The Department of Environmental Conservation's web pages, its spill database and its permit search refuse automated reading, so whether wastewater spills are recorded in the spill database, which covers oil and hazardous substances, could not be checked. Without a duty to tell the public, an overflow reported only to the state leaves no public record, and a missing report cannot be told apart from a quiet month.

**Known problems in the data.** The federal reports give times without a time zone; this site reads them as Alaska time (Hawaii-Aleutian time for a report from the Aleutian Islands west of 169 degrees 30 minutes west, as federal law sets; none so far). Most reports name no receiving water and describe only a manhole or a building. One report names the water and where the sewage went ("Fish Creek which flowed to ... Cook Inlet"); it is shown on Cook Inlet. Fish Creek and Birch Lake in Anchorage and Tongass Narrows in Ketchikan have no water record on this site yet, so the reports that name only them are listed with no water coloured. The Denali report is shown on the Nenana River, the receiving water of the park's treatment plant, which Riley Creek joins nearby. The two Ketchikan bypasses were reported before they began, as planned bypasses.

**What this site does about it.** A report is shown from its start to its end, then with the site's caution (72 hours; 96 on lakes, ponds and reservoirs), since the law sets no period. Of the 46 federal reports, the 6 that name a water or a place on one are shown; the 23 building backups and the 17 that name no water and report no impact to one are not shown on the map or in the lists. Juneau's combined sewer notice page is checked daily for new notices, and its three outfalls are shown at their permitted points. The site's rain caution for combined sewer outfalls uses a radar rainfall product that does not cover Alaska, so Juneau's outfalls show only reported overflows.
