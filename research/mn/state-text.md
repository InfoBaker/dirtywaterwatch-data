**What the law requires.** Minnesota law requires anyone responsible for a discharge that may pollute the state's waters to report it to the Minnesota Pollution Control Agency immediately, through the state Duty Officer (Minn. Stat. 115.061(a)). Since 2023 it also requires a publicly owned treatment plant and any public or private sewer system, promptly after that report, to tell potentially affected downstream users, including the public, Tribal governments and drinking-water facilities, by the fastest means it has (in person, telephone, radio, social media or a web page), to post signs at affected public use areas or tell the body that runs them, and to give the date and time of the discharge, what was released, a warning of the health risk and a contact (115.061(c), widened in 2026). The Agency's guidance names public places within 25 miles downstream and asks for a notice when the release has ended. No law sets a period after a discharge during which the water is to be avoided. Minneapolis's seven combined sewer outfalls are all closed.

**What is published.** The Metropolitan Council, which runs the Twin Cities' regional sewers and treatment plants, posts a page for each spill from its regional sewers and keeps it for one year, with the day it responded, the place, the cause, whether a water was reached, dated updates and the "Do Not Enter/ Sewage Spill" signs it puts up and takes down: three spills on November 14, 2026 (Excelsior in May 2026, which reached Mud Pond; Minneapolis and Golden Valley in July 2026; Eagan in November 2026, where no water was reached). Resource Renew, the Western Lake Superior Sanitary District around Duluth, maps its releases from a public map layer: 117 releases from 2005 to November 4, 2026, 8 of them in 2026, each with its day or time, the kind of structure (manhole, force main, pump station or the treatment plant), an amount as written and whether waters of the state were reached. The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency's overflow records hold one Minnesota report, from the Bois Forte Band's Lake Vermilion sewer system, which EPA permits (June 18, 2024, to Pike Bay).

**What is not published.** The reports made to the Duty Officer are not published, and no statewide list of the public notices the law requires exists: of Minnesota's 642 sewer and treatment operators, two were found to publish their spills in a form this site can read; the others' own channels were not searched one by one. The City of Duluth issues press releases for its overflows, with no list to read. The Council's pages give no time of day, no volume and no end for most spills, and come down after a year; Resource Renew's map gives no end time and no water's name, and leaves the "reached state waters" question blank for 94 of its 117 releases. Lake Superior beach results are posted by the Minnesota Department of Health's program on a site this site could not reach; inland beaches are monitored locally, if at all, and there is no statewide list of bloom advisories.

**What stands in the way.** The law leaves each sewer system to choose how to tell the public, so notices are scattered across telephone calls, signs, radio, social media and individual web pages. The Duty Officer takes reports by telephone only. The Lake Superior beach site and the City of Duluth's site did not answer this site's reader in November 2026. A records request to the Pollution Control Agency is the only route to the statewide reports.

**Known problems in the data.** The Council dates each spill by the day it responded, which may be later than the spill began; a time of day ("afternoon") is not turned into an hour, and an update that says signs "will be removed" does not say on which day they were. Resource Renew's map stores most releases as a date with no time (84 of 117); its amounts mix volumes ("25,000 gallons") with rates ("70 MGD", "100 gpm"), which are not volumes, and words ("UNK", "Bypass"). EPA's one Minnesota report names its water as "Pike Bay Lake Vermilion Tower Minnesota".

**What this site does about it.** A Council spill is shown from the day the Council responded, the only day its notice gives, in Central time; the spill's own start is not given and none is assumed. While the Council's warning signs stand, the spill is shown in the Council's own window. An update saying the signs will be removed does not end that window: the signs are shown standing to the end of that update's day, and after it the end is marked as not confirmed. Once an update says the signs have been removed, the window ends with that update's day, and this site's caution runs 72 hours from the day the spill stopped, so it adds nothing when the signs stood longer; with no stop given, the end stays marked as not confirmed. A spill with no signs is shown with this site's caution for 72 hours after the day it stopped. A spill the Council says reached a water is placed on that water, matched within the county of the town the notice names; a spill that reached no water is listed but placed on none, and a water named only as nearby or found unaffected by the Council's sampling is never coloured. A Resource Renew release is placed by its own map point (kept to about 100 metres), dated to its day or time; a release at the treatment plant is shown as a plant release and the others as sewer overflows; one marked as not reaching state waters is listed and placed on no water; a rate is never counted as a volume. A report dated only to the day, with no end, is shown with this site's caution for 72 hours from the start of that day, then with its end unconfirmed until 30 days after it began.
