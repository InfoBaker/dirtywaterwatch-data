**What the law requires.** Illinois has no statute or rule that requires the public to be told of a sanitary sewer overflow or a treatment plant bypass. Each sewage permit requires the operator to report one to the Illinois Environmental Protection Agency within 24 hours and in writing within five days; those reports are filed through the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency's electronic reporting system. Combined sewer operators must notify the public of overflows under their permits. In the Lake Michigan basin, which in Illinois means the Chicago area (the City of Chicago, the Metropolitan Water Reclamation District of Greater Chicago and 37 suburbs), federal rules add more: public notice by electronic means within four hours of learning of an overflow, signs at outfalls and public access points, notice to the local health department within four hours, a follow-up notice within seven days and an annual notice (40 CFR 122.38). The law sets no period after a discharge during which the water is to be avoided. Beach advisories are issued by county health departments and park districts.

**What is published.** The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency publishes the reports Illinois operators file, daily, with the start and end to the minute, the point, the receiving water and in most cases the volume: about 1,700 reports from 267 permits since October 2024, of which this site reads about 770 as having reached a water (sanitary sewer overflows, combined sewer overflows and bypasses). A report reaches that table a median of three days after the discharge began, and one in ten takes two weeks or more. In Cook County the District publishes a daily map of possible combined sewer overflows at the outfalls it monitors, reset each night, and a table of the times the waterways have been let back into Lake Michigan in severe storms (36 since 1985, the last in July 2023). The Illinois Department of Public Health lists the beach advisories and closures in effect.

**What is not published.** The Chicago area's combined sewer overflows do not appear in the federal reports: the City of Chicago, the District and the suburban permittees file none there. The District's four-hour notices go out only by e-mail and text to people who sign up through a third-party service, and its map marks only unconfirmed activity at monitored outfalls, with no times, volumes or archive; not every outfall is monitored. The District's report of the times each outfall's gate opened and closed, kept for every event day since 2016, is available only through an interactive report form that this site does not read automatically; published as a download or a data feed, it would give every outfall's overflow times. Notices to health departments, the signs at outfalls, and the reports of sanitary sewer overflows to the state are not published. Outside the Chicago area, combined sewer operators' own notices were not found on the web, and only some of them file their overflows with the federal system.

**What stands in the way.** The public's legal channel in the Chicago area is an alert list behind an account; the per-outfall record sits behind a report form; the state's own overflow reports stay with the state agency. A missing report in the federal table cannot be told apart from a quiet week.

**Known problems in the data.** The federal reports give times without a time zone; this site reads them as Central time. Many reports describe the place by a street address and name a basement, a lawn or no water at all; reports that say the sewage stayed out of the water are kept but not placed on one. Some beach advisories on the state's list are years old and still shown as in effect.

**What this site does about it.** A report is shown from its start to its end, then with the site's caution (72 hours; 96 on lakes, ponds and reservoirs), since the law sets no period. A possible overflow on the District's map is shown as an unconfirmed report with no reported end: caution for 14 days, then unresolved to 30 days after it was marked. A reversal into Lake Michigan is shown on the lake for the days the District gives. Beach advisories are shown as the issuing health department or park district gives them, from their start date until the state's list no longer shows them. Street addresses and house numbers in the reports' descriptions are not reproduced here; the full report is in the federal records.
