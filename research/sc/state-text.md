**What the law requires.** South Carolina law requires a wastewater utility to report a significant spill, 5,000 gallons or more of untreated or partially treated sewage, to the Department of Environmental Services by telephone within 24 hours and in writing within five days, and to give public notice of it within 24 hours of discovering it (S.C. Code 48-1-95(B) and (E)). The law names no channel for that notice; the Department gives it when the utility does not. Spills caused by natural disasters, a third party's direct act or other acts of God are excluded. The Department's guidance also asks utilities to report any overflow that reaches a water. No period after a spill is set during which the water is to be avoided. South Carolina has no combined sewers. The Department samples about 122 points on the coast's beaches from May 1 to October 1 and posts swimming advisories, and posts watches and advisories for harmful algal blooms.

**What is published.** The Department publishes the overflow reports utilities file with it in an online list, [View Wastewater Overflows](https://epermitting.des.sc.gov/ext/ncore/external/overflow/list), linked from its [overflow page](https://des.sc.gov/programs/bureau-water/wastewater/wastewater-sewer-overflows). Each report gives the overflow's start and end, the day the Department received the report, the permit and system, the manhole, pump station or pipe, the receiving water, the volume and the cause. On November 16, 2026, the list held 1,773 reports, from September 2022 to November 10, 2026, every one marked completed; a report appeared a median of five days after its overflow began. The City of Florence also posts a notice for each sanitary sewer overflow of 5,000 gallons or more on its [news page](https://www.cityofflorencesc.gov/city-news): five from March 2024 to October 2025 when the page was read on November 14, 2026 (this site's count), all on Jeffries Creek, each giving the day the City learned of or responded to the overflow, its volume or that it was more than 5,000 gallons, and the City's advice. The Department's beach map (the [S.C. Beach Access Guide](https://gis.des.sc.gov/beachaccess/)) shows the current advisory at each of its 123 monitoring stations: on November 14, 2026, by this site's count of the map, 21 long-term advisories, 18 on the Grand Strand in Horry County and 3 at Edisto Beach, which stand from season to season where stormwater makes high bacteria likely after rain; short-term advisories, which usually last a day or two, are posted in season. The Department's [bloom page](https://des.sc.gov/programs/bureau-water/aquatic-science/harmful-algal-blooms) and map listed one watch on November 14, 2026, at Cedar Point Cove on J. Strom Thurmond Reservoir, issued October 16, 2026. The Department's map layer titled "Swimming Advisory" listed 23 river and lake sites on November 15, 2026, among them Cayce Riverwalk, Granby Park and Falls Park, with no date and no status; the Department does not say on the layer what a listing means.

**What is not published.** The Department's list gives no map location for an overflow, only the manhole, pump station or pipe and the county, and it shows a report only once the report is complete, days after the overflow. Other utilities give the required notice in their own ways, and their pages were not searched one by one. The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency's overflow records held no South Carolina report on November 14, 2026. Florence's notices give neither the start nor the end of an overflow, and the City publishes no notice when its advice ends. Neither the beach map nor the "Swimming Advisory" layer gives dates.

**What stands in the way.** The law leaves the public notice to each utility, so there is no one place to learn of an overflow while it happens, and a storm's overflows can fall under the acts-of-God exclusion and need no notice. Charleston Water System posts overflow news on its own site, which did not answer this site's requests in November 2026; it has once rewritten an earlier article for a later break, so its articles cannot be read one per overflow.

**Known problems in the data.** On November 16, 2026, 13 of the Department's 1,773 reports gave a start that cannot be right (after the Department received the report, or years ahead), 58 gave an end before the start and 110 gave the same minute for the start and the end. 490 named no receiving water, and others name only a storm drain, a ditch or "Not Applicable". A few reports were filed twice with the same details. The City of Florence's notices and the Department's list can describe the same overflow. Florence spells the creek both Jeffries and Jefferies. Its figure "more than 5,000 gallons" is a lower bound, and its 972,000 gallons of July 2024 is combined stormwater and wastewater. A Florence notice of October 2025 dates the City's response as "yesterday".

**What this site does about it.** A report on the Department's list is shown with the start, end and volume it gives, in Eastern time, and dated by the Department's receipt as its report time; a start that cannot be right is not shown, and the report is kept without it. An end before the start, an end later than this site's reading of the list, or an end that repeats the start is not used, and the report is shown with its end not confirmed. A report is placed only on the water it names, within the county the list gives; one that names no water, or only a storm drain or a ditch, is listed for South Carolina and colours no water. No point is drawn for a manhole or pump station, since the list gives none. Each report names the system by its permit. A report filed twice with the same details is counted once, and a report that leaves the list stays on this site as a past report. Florence's notices are shown separately from the Department's reports, since the notices give no start to match them by. A Florence notice gives no start for the overflow, so none is shown: the report is dated by the day the City says it received notice of or responded to the overflow, in Eastern time, and shown with this site's caution for 14 days from that day, then with its end not confirmed until 30 days after it. The City's advice to avoid the creek is listed separately on the creek's page, as the City's own and counted with its overflow rather than as another spill, from the day of the notice that carries it, for 14 days after the City last published it, and then as not reconfirmed; when the City's notice no longer carries the advice, it is shown as ended between the two readings of the page, and an ending seen later never extends those 14 days. The notices name no stretch or point for the advice, so it is not drawn on the map and does not colour the creek. The City's figure is kept as given, a lower bound as a lower bound. A notice is placed on the creek it names; no street near a manhole is mapped. A page the City rewrites for a later overflow is kept as a second report, never in place of the first. A beach advisory is shown as the Department's own at its station's point while the map shows it, dated from this site's first reading of the map, since the map gives no date; a station marked "No Advisory" is shown as no report. A site on the "Swimming Advisory" layer is shown as the Department's listing at the layer's point for the site, dated from this site's first reading of the layer, since the layer gives no date, and ended at the first complete reading that no longer lists it; the river or lake around it is not coloured whole. A bloom watch or advisory is shown as the Department's own at the centre of the area the Department delineated, never as the whole lake or reservoir, since the Department names a cove or arm; where two delineations on one water share a date and nothing ties each to its cove, no point is given, and the watch is listed on the reservoir's page with its place on the map not known. South Carolina has no combined sewer outfalls, so this site's rain caution does not apply here.
