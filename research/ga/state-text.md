Georgia's rule on sewage spills (Ga. Comp. R. & Regs. 391-3-6-.05) applies to every publicly owned sewer system that spills raw sewage into the state's waters. The operator must tell the Georgia Environmental Protection Division (EPD) immediately, report the spill to local television, radio and newspapers within 24 hours and to the local health department immediately, and post signs at the spill and at bridges, trails, boat ramps and other public access points downstream for at least seven days after the spill ends. A spill of more than 10,000 gallons also needs a notice in the county's legal newspaper within seven days. The rule sets no period after a spill during which the public is told to keep out of the water, so Georgia reports carry this site's 72-hour caution rather than an official window. The signs, the broadcast notices and the newspaper notices are not published online.

EPD posts a list of municipal sewage spills each business day, covering about the last 30 days. It gives the day a spill began, the permit, the street location, the gallons (often left blank or zero until the amount is determined), the river basin and the stream affected; it gives no time of day and no end, and rows have no identifier, so this site keeps each day's list to build a history and keeps spills after they leave EPD's 30-day window. Rows that EPD corrects or removes are kept with a note. Points come from EPD's Spills in Georgia map, matched by permit and address. The same spills reach the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency through Georgia's electronic reporting system; EPA's copy carries start and end times but no volume, and where both describe the same spill on the same day, EPA's report is shown and EPD's row is counted once. EPA's reports often give the sewer system's own location rather than the spill's, and those locations are not used as spill points. Many reports name a small stream or an unnamed tributary that is not yet mapped here.

Georgia has four combined sewer permits (Atlanta's East and West areas, Columbus and Albany). Atlanta's permits require overflow notices on signs and on the city's website; no dedicated overflow page was found, though the city has announced at least one overflow in a press release. No public overflow notices were found for Columbus or Albany. On the coast, the Department of Natural Resources' Healthy Beaches program posts temporary advisories when bacteria levels are elevated and keeps permanent advisories at a few beaches; inland lakes and rivers are not monitored statewide, and no statewide algal bloom feed was found.
