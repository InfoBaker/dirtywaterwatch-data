**What the law requires.** No Ohio statute or rule requires the public to be told of a sewage discharge in general. Operators report sewer overflows and bypasses to Ohio EPA within 24 hours under their permits, and those reports are not published. Ohio's permit rule (OAC 3745-33-08) requires a permanent sign at every permitted outfall, combined sewer overflow and bypass outfalls included, and requires a major discharger near a drinking-water intake to notify the water supplier as soon as practicable after a discharge begins. Combined sewer operators (sewage and rainwater in one pipe) in the Lake Erie basin must give the public an initial notice within four hours of becoming aware of an overflow under their notification plans (40 CFR 122.38); those in the Ohio River basin notify under their permits and consent decrees. No Ohio rule sets a period after a discharge during which the water should be avoided. The Northeast Ohio Regional Sewer District's page says public health officials advise against recreational contact for at least 48 hours after a rain event when an overflow occurs, and the Metropolitan Sewer District of Greater Cincinnati advises avoiding contact with streams for 72 hours after rain.

**What is published.** There is no statewide list of sewer overflows. The Northeast Ohio Regional Sewer District's map gives, for each of its 112 combined sewer outfalls (on November 8, 2026) in and around Cleveland, the start, end and volume of the last wet-weather and dry-weather overflow, updated hourly; 24 outfalls are metered and the district's model gives the others. Akron's map shows whether each of its 28 sites (on November 8, 2026) is overflowing; the city notes the real-time data has not been reviewed for accuracy. Columbus publishes every overflow it has recorded at its 52 discharge points since 2002, 8,542 on November 8, 2026 (291 in 2026, the newest September 29), each with the day it was found at inspection and, on most, the volume; the city updates them monthly, usually after the 15th, so a record can appear weeks after the overflow. Bowling Green lists its overflows with start, end and estimated volume (37 from 2019 to July 21, 2026), and Clyde's yearly reports list each overflow of its one outfall on Raccoon Creek the same way (9 from 2017 to April 1, 2026). Ohio EPA's spills layer lists the spills called in to its emergency line since 2017 with the day, a point and the time of the call: 1,121 reports of human sewage, sewage sludge or biosolids, or waste water to November 5, 2026. The Ohio Department of Health's BeachGuard lists the bacteria and algal-bloom advisories in effect at bathing beaches (five on November 8, 2026: four algal-bloom warnings at Grand Lake St. Marys since May 20 and one bacteria advisory since July 31).

**What is not published.** Ohio EPA publishes none of the 24-hour overflow reports it receives, and Ohio files none to the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency's national overflow records. Ohio EPA's spills layer holds only incidents called in to the emergency line; it names no operator, gives no start time or end, and names a water on 218 of the 1,121. A report Ohio EPA files as waste water does not say whether it was sewage or industrial wastewater. The Northeast Ohio Regional Sewer District's map keeps only the latest overflow at each outfall, so earlier ones are gone once a newer one is posted. Columbus's records give no end and no time of day. Most smaller combined sewer communities publish only an annual report, or nothing online.

**What stands in the way.** The Metropolitan Sewer District of Greater Cincinnati, with 199 combined sewer outfalls, shows its overflows only on an embedded dashboard with no data to read. Lakewood's map shows only current status, with no list of overflows. Lima publishes its notices and Toledo its reports only as documents, one per overflow or per report, not as a list. Ohio's algal-bloom information site could not be reached on November 3, 2026. Without a state requirement, what is published depends on each operator.

**Known problems in the data.** The Northeast Ohio Regional Sewer District publishes its map data "for display purposes only"; some of its records give the same minute for a dry-weather overflow's start and end with no volume, and some give a whole day with a total duration. Columbus's date is the day an overflow was found at inspection, so it may have begun earlier. Many spills on Ohio EPA's layer give no amount, and its count includes sludge and biosolids spills, which can be from a truck or a field rather than a sewer.

**What this site does about it.** The Northeast Ohio Regional Sewer District's and Akron's maps are read hourly and each new overflow is kept as a dated record, so their history grows from November 8, 2026; each record says whether the district metered or modelled it. If an update is missed and the district's map has moved on to a newer overflow at the same outfall, the earlier one is shown as ended no later than the newer one began. A report with an end is shown to its end, then with this site's caution (72 hours; 96 on lakes, ponds and reservoirs), since the law sets no period. A report dated to the day only, with no end (Ohio EPA's spills, Columbus's records), shows caution for 72 hours from the start of that day, then is marked "End not confirmed" until 30 days after it began. An Akron overflow is dated to the readings that first and last showed it. Ohio EPA's spills are shown as reports called in to the state, not as an operator's confirmation, and a waste water report is shown as a possible sewage release with its product named. A beach advisory is shown while BeachGuard lists it, quoted as the issuing agency's. Elsewhere, the combined sewer outfalls show this site's rain caution after heavy rain.
