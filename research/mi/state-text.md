**What the law requires.** Under Michigan's Natural Resources and Environmental Protection Act (MCL 324.3112a), whoever is responsible for a sewer system that discharges untreated or partially treated sewage onto land or into the state's waters must notify the Department of Environment, Great Lakes, and Energy (EGLE), the local health departments and a daily newspaper immediately, and within 24 hours of the discharge's start. When the discharge ends, the operator reports its volume and quality, its cause, the waters or land it reached and when it began and ended, and it must test the affected waters for E. coli and send the results to the county health departments and EGLE. EGLE must then promptly post each notification on its website. Combined sewer operators in the Great Lakes basin, which is the whole state, must also give the public notice within four hours under federal rules; the Great Lakes Water Authority, the state's largest, gives that notice through EGLE's list. The law sets no period after a discharge during which the water is to be avoided; local health departments decide on beach advisories from their own sampling.

**What is published.** EGLE's MiEnviro list of sewage discharges carries every report: combined sewer overflows, sanitary sewer overflows, discharges from retention treatment basins (combined sewage that is settled and disinfected before it overflows) and other discharges from sewers and treatment plants. Each report gives the operator, the permit, the county, the start and end to the minute, the kind of sewage and, once the report is completed, the volume and the receiving water; combined sewer reports usually give the outfall number. Reports appear when the operator files them, a median of about 15 hours after the discharge began; more than a third arrive after 24 hours. On 4 November 2026 the list held 332 reports still in process and this site had read 2,866 completed reports since January 2025. The list reaches back to 2001.

**What is not published.** Where a discharge happened: the list gives no map point, so this site places a report by its permit and outfall number, or by the receiving water it names. A report in process often names no receiving water and no volume (144 of the 332 open reports named no water). The newspaper notices are printed only. The notices to health departments and the E. coli results the law requires are not published. There is no e-mail alert for sewage discharges; EGLE offers e-mail alerts for beach advisories only. Beach sample results and advisory details sit behind a login.

**What stands in the way.** The law's own public channel is a printed newspaper notice; EGLE's posting is the only web record, and the service behind it has no documentation and no stated licence. Michigan's BeachGuard map shows a current colour per beach, but the advisories and results behind it need an account.

**Known problems in the data.** A report in process repeats the start time as its end until it is completed, so this site reads such a report as having no reported end. Some reports stay in process for years, and a few carry mistyped years. Permit numbers are typed by the filer in many forms, and some first notices give none. The same report is occasionally filed twice; this site keeps the one EGLE received last.

**What this site does about it.** A report with no reported end is shown as ongoing for seven days and then as unresolved, with no end reported, until 30 days after it began. A completed report is shown from its start to its end, then with 72 hours of caution, since the law sets no period. Retention treatment basin discharges are shown as combined sewer overflows of partially treated sewage. A completed report that names only the land the sewage reached is kept but not placed on a water. Spills of fuel, oil or chemicals and spills of sludge onto land are counted but not shown as sewage reports. The narrative fields of each report are not reproduced here, because they can name the people involved and residents' addresses; the full report is on EGLE's list.
