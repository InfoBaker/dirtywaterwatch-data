**What the law requires.** Every sewer overflow or other unauthorized discharge must be reported to the Texas Commission on Environmental Quality's regional office as soon as possible and within 24 hours, and in writing within five working days; small spills that meet the Commission's conditions may instead go in a monthly summary (30 TAC 305.125(9)). A local government's facility must also tell local government officials and local media within 24 hours of a spill likely to affect a source of drinking water, of 50,000 gallons or more near one or in a sensitive area such as an aquifer recharge zone, or of 100,000 gallons or more, with a warning to avoid contact with the spill, the soil and the water in the affected area (30 TAC 319.302 and 319.303). Nothing requires a spill to be posted on the web, and the law sets no period after a spill during which the water is to be avoided. Texas has no combined sewers. Texas Beach Watch, run by the General Land Office with federal beach grants, samples the coastal beaches; local governments post its advisories.

**What is published.** The Commission publishes the reports it receives as open data: 56,545 reports of spills that began from September 2, 2010 to August 29, 2026 (read November 7, 2026), 4,320 of them begun in the twelve months to August 2026, with the start date, the date the Commission received the report, the volume, the cause, the county, the operator and its permits, and until July 2024 the end date. The City of Houston's plants and sewers account for 1,261 of those 4,320. A report reaches the Commission a median of 2 days after the spill began. One report in four gives a receiving water, and about one in eight gives a water's own name; the others say "not provided", give only a segment number, or say the spill reached the ground, a street or a storm drain. Texas Beach Watch publishes each of its 174 coastal stations' latest enterococcus result with the program's level: Low, Medium, or High, at which it recommends an advisory (on November 7, 2026, 12 stations at Medium and none at High), and sends e-mail alerts by beach.

**What is not published.** The Commission's table gives no times of day and no locations: no coordinates, and street addresses only inside the operator's description. It gives no publication date for a report and is refreshed in batches; the copy read on November 7, 2026 was last updated on September 24, 2026 and held reports received up to August 31, so the newest report had begun 70 days earlier. The notices to local officials and media under 30 TAC 319.302 are not collected anywhere public. Houston publishes monthly overflow reports under its 2021 federal consent decree as PDF files (through September 2026 on November 7, 2026), with times, addresses and whether the sewage reached a water; Austin and Dallas post news releases on large overflows; San Antonio's and El Paso's water utilities post none, and Fort Worth's site refuses automated reading. No state program of advisories for freshwater algal blooms was found. The other operators' own pages were not searched one by one.

**What stands in the way.** The only public record of a spill is the Commission's table, months after the spill; nothing reaches the public on the day unless a spill is large enough for the notice to local media and a newspaper or station carries it. The table names the water in a minority of reports and never gives a point, so most reports cannot be tied to a water from the record alone. Galveston County's beach advisory page refuses automated reading.

**Known problems in the data.** Every date in the table is written as midnight, so the times of day are unknown. The table has given no end date for any report received since July 22, 2024: 9,594 reports have none. 44 give an end date that cannot be right (in the years 2030 to 3000); this site does not count those as ended. Some reports give the receiving water as a Commission segment number or a river basin ("Colorado" for spills in Midland and Big Spring) rather than a water, and the same water is often spelled several ways ("Burnett Bay", "Burnet Bay"). 89 report numbers appear twice. A small number of reports name a material other than sewage. Texas Beach Watch publishes only each station's latest sample, not the day an advisory began or ended.

**What this site does about it.** A report is shown on the whole water it names, from its start date to its end date, then with this site's caution (72 hours; 96 on lakes, ponds and reservoirs), since the law sets no period; a report with no end date is shown with this site's caution for 72 hours from the start of the day the spill began, then with its end not confirmed until 30 days after it began. A report that names no water, or names only a segment number or a river basin, is listed with its county and is not placed on any water: this site does not place it at the operator's plant and does not guess a location from the description. This site carries the reports begun since January 1, 2023 (16,372 on November 7, 2026); the earlier reports remain in the Commission's table. A report that says the sewage stayed on land is not shown on a water. Dates are read in Central time, and in Mountain time in El Paso and Hudspeth counties. The table is read again whenever the Commission refreshes it. Beach Watch's High level is shown as the program's advisory for as long as the program's map shows it; its Medium level, at which the program recommends no advisory, is shown with this site's caution from the sample date. House numbers and personal details in the reports' descriptions are not reproduced here; the full reports are in the Commission's open data.
