North Carolina has no combined sewers; its reported sewage discharges are sanitary sewer overflows and bypasses. State law (G.S. 143-215.1C) requires the operator of a wastewater system to send a press release to the news media covering the county within 24 hours of finding that 1,000 gallons or more of untreated wastewater reached surface waters, and to publish a newspaper notice for 15,000 gallons or more. Neither notice has to be posted online, and the law sets no period after a discharge during which the water should be avoided, so this site applies its own caution once a discharge ends. Operators must also report each overflow to the Division of Water Resources within 24 hours, and the Division publishes these reports on its public Sanitary Sewer Overflow Tracking Map. That map is the record shown here, from September 2022 onward. Privately owned systems (utility companies, property owners' associations, mobile-home parks and industrial sites) are bound by the same law and are listed like public ones, under the permittee the Division's permit records name; a few reports name no permit and are shown without an operator. Each report gives the start and end times, the estimated volume and the volume that reached water, the stream as the Division names it, and a location. The map keeps one start, end and volume per report. For reports through October 2026, where the operator's written five-day report differed from the first report, the site used the earlier start, the later end and the larger volume. Entries can reach the map a few days after the event. Some reports give no end time, and the 2022 to 2025 history gives none. The map does not show whether the press release was sent. On the coast, the Division of Marine Fisheries posts swimming advisories and storm-water precautionary advisories for its monitoring stations. Its map shows only the current status, with no issue or lift dates, so the site dates each advisory from when it saw the map change: these dates are the site's own observation, not dates published by the Division, and an advisory may have been issued earlier. When an advisory has not been reconfirmed for 14 days, the site stops colouring it. The Division also closes coastal shellfish harvest areas by dated proclamation because of bacteriological water quality, and reopens them by a later proclamation. We show each temporary closure from its effective time to the effective day of the proclamation that reopens the area, marked at a point inside the area where the Division's map outlines it; the water around it is not coloured. A closure that no later proclamation reopens is coloured for 14 days, then listed as not reconfirmed. Changes to permanently closed areas are not shown. A shellfish closure is an order on harvesting, separate from the swimming advisories. Algal bloom reports to the Division of Water Resources are shown as reports, not advisories, for seven days after the bloom was seen; their locations are rounded to about 100 metres. A water with no report is grey, which means only that nothing has been reported.
