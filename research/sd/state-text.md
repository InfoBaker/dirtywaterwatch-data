**What the law requires.** No South Dakota statute or rule was found that requires the public to be told of a sewage overflow or a treatment plant bypass. Federal rules require every sewage permit to carry a report of such an event within 24 hours and a written report within five days (40 CFR 122.41(l)(6)); in South Dakota the reports go to the Department of Agriculture and Natural Resources, and to the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency for the permits it writes on tribal lands. The permit texts themselves were not read. The written reports are filed through the federal agency's electronic reporting system. The state's spill rule, which requires a suspected discharge to be reported within 24 hours, covers fuels, chemicals and other regulated substances; the department's spill page does not name sewage. Under the federal policy for combined sewers (sewage and rainwater in one pipe), the permits of the City of Lead and the Lead-Deadwood Sanitary District are expected to require public notice of overflows; no notice from either was found online. No period after a discharge is set during which the water is to be avoided. The department issues harmful algal bloom advisories for a whole lake when test results show high-risk conditions or a person or animal is confirmed to have been affected; in its words, "Advisories expire at the end of the calendar year."

**What is published.** The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency publishes the written reports South Dakota operators file, daily, with the start and end to the minute, the point, in most cases the volume, and the receiving water when the operator names one: 208 reports from 56 permits, April 2023 to November 2, 2026 (the federal file of November 4, 2026), of which this site reads 57 as having reached a water, from 30 permits (sanitary sewer overflows and bypasses, and the City of Lead's overflows to Gold Run Creek). A report reaches that file a median of five days after the discharge began; one in ten takes 57 days or more. The department's algal bloom page lists the year's advisories with the lake, its county and a date: five lakes in 2026 as of November 7, 2026.

**What is not published.** The department posts no list of the overflow reports it receives, and the 24-hour reports are not published anywhere. No operator was found to post its overflows on the web; Sioux Falls announced its June 2024 flood releases to the Big Sioux River at a press conference. Of about 500 sewage permits in the state, 56 have a report in the federal file, so a permit with no report there cannot be told apart from one that does not file. No program that samples swimming beaches for bacteria and posts the results was found. The department's bloom map, in its own words, "does not show current conditions", and its yearly advisory map layers stop at 2024.

**What stands in the way.** With no public-notice duty, the only public trace of an overflow is the written report in a federal database, days to weeks after the event. A same-day posting of the 24-hour reports, by the department or by the operators, would need a change in law or an agency decision.

**Known problems in the data.** The federal reports give times without a time zone, and South Dakota keeps two: this site reads a report in Mountain time when its point lies in one of the fifteen counties west of the federal time-zone boundary (49 CFR 71.7), or in the part of Stanley County west of that boundary as the U.S. Department of Transportation's time-zone map draws it, and in Central time otherwise, Fort Pierre's corner of Stanley County included; by that rule 71 of the 208 reports are in Mountain time. Many reports describe a basement backup, a spill onto the ground or into a treatment lagoon cell, or say "Does not apply"; those are left off the map. Eighteen reports cover a flood or other extreme event and give only the dates of the period. The City of Lead files its combined sewer overflows as sanitary sewer overflows at a manhole. Receiving-water names are typed freely ("Unnamed", "tributary to mud creek"). Two planned bypasses run for months: Rapid City's bypass of its ultraviolet disinfection building to Rapid Creek, from November 2, 2026 to April 30, 2027 (about 1.66 billion gallons as reported), and Aberdeen's bypasses of parts of its treatment plant to Moccasin Creek during its rebuild, from March 23, 2026 to June 1, 2028.

**What this site does about it.** A report is shown from its start to its end, then with the site's caution (72 hours; 96 on lakes, ponds and reservoirs), since the law sets no period. A report with no end is shown with caution for 14 days from the report, then as unresolved to 30 days after it began. A report of an extreme event is dated to the days it covers. A report that says the sewage stayed out of the water is left off the map. A planned bypass is shown as ongoing to the end its operator planned, as the operator gave it. Algal bloom advisories are shown as blooms, not as sewage, from the date the department lists to the end of that calendar year, the department's own period. Street addresses and house numbers in the reports' descriptions are not reproduced here; the full report is in the federal records.
